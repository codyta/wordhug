export const text = "Mẹo chọn đồ giúp bạn [ăn mặc] đẹp hơn mỗi ngày";

wordhug started with Vietnamese. Many Vietnamese words are compounds of several syllables written with spaces between them, like *ăn mặc* (to dress), *học sinh* (student) or *Hà Nội*. To a browser every syllable is a separate word, so a line can break in the middle of one, even in a nicely balanced heading:

<div class="demo-row">
  <Demo label="Without wordhug" width={30}>
    <p style="text-wrap: balance"><Plain text={text} /></p>
  </Demo>
  <Demo label="With wordhug" width={30}>
    <p style="text-wrap: balance"><WordHug text={text} /></p>
  </Demo>
</div>

"Outfit tips to help you dress better every day" can open with *Mẹo chọn đồ giúp bạn ăn*, "outfit tips to help you eat". A bad break doesn't just look untidy; it can change what the sentence says.

## Why not a line break?

Content writers and designers usually know exactly where a line should and shouldn't break. The common fix has been a hard `<br />`, but that:

- **mixes presentation into content:** a visual choice becomes part of the text's markup and meaning;
- **fits only one layout:** a break placed for a wide screen lands in the wrong spot on a phone;
- **makes copying messy:** copied text picks up stray line breaks.

wordhug flips the approach: instead of saying where to break, you mark what belongs together. The text still wraps naturally at any width, just never inside a group. It works with `text-wrap: balance` too: the browser still balances the lines, treating each group as a single word.

## Not just Vietnamese

The same applies in any language: names, prices, dates and short phrases that read as one unit.

<Demo label="With wordhug" width={16}>
  <p><WordHug text="Meet [Dr. Jane Doe] at [10:30 am], tickets [only $9]" /></p>
</Demo>
